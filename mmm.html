Case study  · Marketing mix modeling

MMM: A Model to Measure Media Efficiency

A Bayesian marketing mix model built in Google Meridian, to judge cross-channel efficiency and guide budget allocation — shown here on 104 weeks of synthetic data.

Problem

Channels don't add up

At a jewellery brand: attribution models disagreed with each other, and offline effectiveness was untrackable.

Approach + Result

MAPE 9%, gap 24.9%

Bayesian MMM in Google Meridian — accurate enough to size the budget, not yet to move it between channels.

Cadence

Quarterly refit

The weakest channel each cycle sets the next quarter's test.

My role

Solo, end-to-end

Data audit, model spec, diagnostics, optimisation, validation.

AI's role

Automates the checks

Scope calls and next steps stay with me.

How it was built

Six steps from raw data to a validated result

  1. 01

    Data audit

    Detected media, impression and KPI columns automatically, then checked spend share, correlation and VIF for identifiability risk.

  2. 02

    Scope decisions

    Tested whether to split to ad-group level and include offline spend; excluded what failed collinearity or holdout checks and moved it to experiments.

    See the decisions ↓
  3. 03

    Bayesian model

    Meridian with a log-normal ROI prior (mean 2, sd 1.5) and adstock up to 7 weeks, sampled with 5 chains × 2,000 draws.

  4. 04

    Diagnostics

    Checked trace plots and R-hat, then reviewed contribution, ROI, response curves and adstock decay.

  5. 05

    Optimisation

    Reallocated the historical budget at a fixed total with ±30% channel constraints to maximise incremental revenue.

  6. 06

    Validation

    Compared estimated ROI with a prior ROI benchmark to measure attribution error, separately from fit.

Inside step 02: the two scope decisions

Should the model go down to ad-group level?

The question
Would splitting each platform into ad groups give more useful ROI estimates than one number per channel?
Why it is hard
More granular inputs multiply the number of media parameters against a fixed number of weekly observations, and ad groups inside one platform usually move together, so their effects become hard to separate.
How I tested it
  • Pairwise spend correlation and VIF across candidate ad groups.
  • Spend variation per ad group (coefficient of variation over time).
  • Out-of-sample fit on a holdout balanced across time, not one block at the end of the window, comparing in-sample vs. holdout error for the channel-level and ad-group models.
  • Whether posteriors move away from the priors or simply return them.
Decision rule
Keep the more granular spec only if collinearity is acceptable and holdout error does not degrade. Thresholds: [VIF THRESHOLD], [MAX ACCEPTABLE HOLDOUT ERROR GAP]. If the rule fails, keep the model at channel level, exclude ad-group splits from the MMM, and measure them with in-platform experiments (A/B or conversion-lift tests).
Outcome
I tested the ad-group split in an earlier iteration. Ad groups within the same platform moved together too closely, so the multicollinearity was too high for the model to separate their effects. I kept the model at channel level and left ad-group questions to in-platform experiments.

Should offline channel budgets be included?

The question
Should offline spend enter the model as a media channel alongside digital?
Why it is hard
Offline spend is often flighted in a few bursts that coincide with seasonal peaks, so its effect can be confounded with seasonality and baseline, and there are few independent data points.
How I tested it
The same four checks as the ad-group decision, plus whether offline flights overlap with seasonal peaks or with large digital pushes.
Decision rule
Include offline only if it passes the same thresholds. If it fails, exclude it from the MMM, estimate it with a geo experiment (test vs. control regions), and feed the result back into the MMM as an ROI prior.
Outcome
[WHAT I DECIDED AND WHY] In the final model, offline discounting enters as a control variable, not as a media channel.

Excluding a channel from the model is a measurement decision, not a statement that the channel does not work.

The reason is channel identifiability, not a broken model. When channels are scaled up and down together, the model can tell how much revenue media drives in total, but not which channel drove it — that's a mathematical limit on what the data can separate, independent of whether the model is well built. The data audit flagged high VIF across the media inputs before fitting. The model then spread credit across channels: all three posterior ROIs landed close to each other and close to the model's own fitting prior (mean 2), while Google's prior ROI benchmark was 3.00. In practice: I'd trust this model to size next quarter's total media budget — the channel-level split is the one piece worth testing before acting on it.

Note: recovery error is computed in the notebook from the rounded ROI shown in Meridian's summary table (Meta 1.8, Google 1.9). The unrounded posterior means are 1.83 and 1.94, so the exact errors are slightly lower. The conclusion does not change.

The decision

Hold the channel split, size the total budget, and test Google before moving it

What the model suggests

Shift about $65k from Meta into Google

The optimiser moves +$65k into Google, funded mostly by Meta, for an expected gain of about +$6k on $3M of incremental revenue.

Why I'm holding off

It leans on the one estimate I haven't verified

Google has the largest gap against its prior ROI (36.7%), and its credible interval runs from 0.9 to 3.3. The expected gain is small next to that uncertainty — not proof the model is wrong, just reason enough to check before moving money.

What I'm doing instead

Run a lift test, then feed it back

Run a lift experiment on Google first. Use the measured incremental ROI as an informative ROI prior for that channel in Meridian, refit, and only then re-run the optimiser.

Limits and next steps

What this does not show yet

Why I think R² of 0.30 doesn't affect the credibility of this model

R² measures how much of the week-to-week variance in revenue the model explains; MAPE measures the average absolute error on each week's prediction — they penalise the model differently. Baseline (non-media) revenue accounts for 68.1% of total contribution, and that baseline's own week-to-week movement is harder for a model built on media regressors to fully capture. That caps how much variance the model can claim credit for (R²), even while its absolute prediction error stays low (MAPE). A low R² here is a property of how much of revenue is baseline versus media-driven, not evidence the model fits poorly.

Adapting this to a brand-led, distributor-sold business

Three changes, in priority order, plus one that applies regardless. First: get sell-out (POS) data instead of sell-in. Sell-in reflects the distributor's own ordering and inventory decisions — stocking cycles, trade deals, quarter-end pushes — not real consumer demand, which is why it doesn't work well as an MMM's dependent variable; sell-out, pulled from a retail partner's POS system or bought as syndicated data, is the real sales signal and the industry default. Second: trade promotion needs its own variable, separate from media. Retailer discounts, displays and trade deals often move sell-out more than media does, and a promotion also pulls forward future purchases — a dip right after the promotion that a naive model would misread as decay rather than borrowed demand. Third, only if POS data genuinely isn't available: fall back to a brand metric media can move directly — brand mention volume, or branded search — and model the link from that metric to sell-through as a separate step. Alongside all three: lengthen the adstock window. This project's adstock decays over 7 weeks because an e-commerce purchase follows exposure quickly; a distributor-sold brand has a longer, lumpier path from exposure to an actual sale, so the carryover window needs to stretch to match it.

Why TikTok having no prior ROI doesn't affect whether this model works

TikTok is fit with exactly the same mechanism as Meta and Google — the same shared prior, the same MCMC process — and it converges and produces a usable estimate (2.0 ROI, a share of total contribution) like any other channel. Meridian doesn't need an external benchmark to fit a channel; only this case study's validation step does. The missing prior ROI is a gap in what I can check for that one channel, not a gap in the model itself. Separately: because all three channels are estimated jointly from correlated spend, TikTok's result still interacts with Meta's and Google's — that's the collinearity finding above, not a consequence of the missing benchmark.
